## Summary
This course project explores the relationship between a country’s economic wealth and the success of its professional men's tennis players, as measured through the Association of Tennis Professionals (ATP) rankings and common player performance metrics. The motivation for this analysis stems from the observation and mutual understanding that professional tennis is a resource-intensive sport, requiring large financial investments in training, coaching, travel, and competition over the course of a few years to several decades. As a result, players from wealthier countries may have certain structural advantages that contribute to higher performance outcomes in matchplay. This project seeks to determine whether such advantages are observable and significant in real-world data and whether macroeconomic indicators such as GDP can help explain some of the variation in ATP rankings.

The central research question that is guiding this project is: *"Is there a relationship between a country’s wealth, measured by GDP, and the likelihood of producing top-ranked ATP players?"* To address this question, we integrated three complementary datasets from a variety of creadible sources: an ATP players dataset containing demographics and physical characteristics, an ATP rankings dataset capturing player performance over time, and a country-level macroeconomic dataset providing GDP and other related indicators and statistics. By combining these three datasets, we were able to link individual player performance with national economic conditions, which would serve as the basis for the rest of our insight.

The analysis began with extensive data preprocessing to ensure consistency and usability across datasets. This included converting improperly formatted date fields, appropriately handling NaN/missing values, standardizing country identifiers, and removing irrelevant or inconsistent entries such as obsolete countries or aggregated regions. Once cleaned, the datasets were merged using player IDs and country-level mappings, resulting in a dataset that was suitable for further analysis.

Exploratory data analysis revealed that both GDP and player performance metrics were highly skewed. Since this was not ideal for us to move forward with, log transformations were utilized to better visualize relationships. Various visualizations, such as scatter plots, box plots, and aggregated country-level comparisons, were used to examine patterns and trends in the data. These analyses indicated a weak but positive relationship between GDP and player performance. To our understanding, this suggested that while wealthier countries tend to produce stronger players on average, the relationship is not strong or deterministic and cannot be directly associated with causation.

To further investigate this relationship, we implemented a linear regression model to predict player ranking outcomes based on both player characteristics and economic indicators such as GDP. The model demonstrated somewhat limited predictive power, indicating that GDP and the available player features alone are insufficient to accurately predict rankings. This suggests that tennis performance is influenced by a much broader set of factors not captured in the datasets we examined to support answering our research question. These could include, but are not limited to, coaching quality, habitual training enviroment and resources, and individual talent.

Overall, this project highlights both the notable potential and the cautionary limitations of integrating data to study sports performance. While economic wealth has an effect, it is only a small piece of a much more complex system that requires further analysis over time.

## Data Profile
This project utilizes three primary datasets that together provide a diverse perspective on professional tennis performance, combining individual player attributes, match outcomes, and country-level economic indicators. The integration of these datasets allows for a thorough analysis that links micro-level athlete characteristics with macro-level economic conditions.

The first dataset, the ATP Players Dataset from Jeff Sackmann (atp_players.csv), contains 65,989 rows and 8 columns and serves as the foundational dataset for player-level demographics and physical attributes. Each row represents a unique player and is identified by a player_id, which acts as a key for linking across datasets. The dataset includes variables such as first and last name, dominant hand (e.g. left, right, ambidextrous, or unknown), date of birth, country code (IOC format), and height in centimeters. One notable characteristic of this dataset is that it spans several decades of professional tennis history. This wide coverage introduces both strengths and challenges worth nothing. While it provides a rich pool of player information, it also includes outdated or incomplete records, particularly for historical or less popular players where attributes such as height or handedness may be missing. Additionally, the date of birth field is stored in a non-standard numeric format, requiring transformation before it can be used for our analysis. This aside, the dataset is useful because it provides the variables necessary to explore how individual player characteristics may relate to performance outcomes over an extended period of time.

The second dataset is also from Jeff Sackmann. This ATP Rankings Dataset (atp_rankings_current.csv), contains 92,341 rows and 4 columns and captures weekly ranking updates for players during the 2024 tennis calender year. Each record includes a ranking date, player rank, player ID, and ATP ranking points, which are accumulated based on performance in ATP Challenger, ATP 250, ATP 500, Masters 1000, and Grand Slam tournaments. Unlike the players dataset, this dataset has a time-series structure, meaning that each player can appear multiple times across different weeks. This allows for a hollistic view of player performance over time. The use of the 2024 season specifically was a deliberate choice to ensure that the analysis reflects a current competitive landscape of professional tennis, rather than being influenced by historical changes in the sport. However, this also introduces limitations, as rankings represent discrete snapshots rather than continuous performance and may not fully capture fluctuations between ranking periods. The rankings dataset is essential because it provides the primary outcome variables, rank and points, that serve as quantitative measures of overall player success in professional tennis.

The third dataset, the USDA International Macroeconomic Dataset, is publicaly provided by the USDA. It introduces a macroeconomic dimension to the analysis by providing country-level indicators, most notably Real GDP in U.S. dollars. This dataset spans from approximately 1970 to 2035, and includes both historical observations and forward-looking projections. It contains data for a large array of countries, across the globe, covering nearly all nations represented in men's professional tennis and the ATP dataset. The inclusion of GDP allows us to best estimate a country’s overall economic capacity, which can be interpreted as a proxy for resources available for athletes. However, the dataset also includes aggregated regional entries (such as “Asia Less Japan”) and projected values, both of which introduce potential inconsistencies in analytical conclusion-making. These entries were removed or handled carefully to ensure alignment with player-level data. Additionally, because GDP is measured at the country level, it represents an average or aggregate condition rather than the specific circumstances of individual tennis players.

The integration of these datasets required linking across two different types of identifiers. The ATP players and rankings datasets were merged using the player_id, which provides a direct and reliable connection between player attributes and performance metrics. However, linking the tennis data to the GDP dataset required a more complex approach, as it involved mapping three-letter IOC country codes to full country names used in the macroeconomic dataset. This mapping step was crucial for ensuring that each player was correctly associated with the appropriate economic data for their country of representation.

From a storage and organization perspective, all datasets are maintained within the project repository in a structured directory format. This organization ensures that the original data remains unchanged while allowing cleaned and transformed versions to be used for analysis. It also supports reproducibility by supporting full data transparency.

In terms of ethical and legal considerations, the ATP datasets are distributed under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike license, which permits their use for academic and/or research purposes but restricts commercial applications. As mentioned previously, the USDA macroeconomic dataset is publicly available and falls within the public domain, making it freely usable for all people. Importantly, none of the datasets contain sensitive personal information beyond publicly known athlete attributes, minimizing privacy concerns. However, an important analytical limitation arises from the combination of individual-level and country-level data, which introduces the potential for ecological bias. While GDP can provide insight into national-level trends, it does not directly represent individual players.

These combined datasets enable us to explore whether a country’s economic strength acts as a “multiplier” for the development and success of its tennis players, while also highlighting the complexities and limitations in combining data across levels of aggregation.

## Data Quality
The data quality assessment was a key component of this project, as the reliability and validity of any analysis depend heavily on the integrity and transparency of the underlying data. Across the three datasets used in this project, we identified several categories of data quality issues, including missing values, inconsistent formatting, mismatched identifiers, skewed distributions, outdated or invalid entries, and more. Each of these issues required careful evaluation and impactful solutions to ensure that the final dataset was suitable for meaningful analysis both in the present and in the future for other data scientists.

One of the most prevalent issues that we observed was missing values. In the ATP players dataset, key attributes such as height and handedness were incomplete for a subset of players, mainly those who were active a long time ago or were not as popular. While these variables were not the primary focus of the research question, they still contributed to the overall exploratory analysis and modeling. Missing values in these columns could reduce statistical power or introduce bias if certain types of players were more likely to have incomplete records. In the GDP dataset, missing values appeared for certain countries and years, largely in cases where economic data was unavailable or not reported. This posed a more significant issue, as GDP was a central variable in our analysis. To address this, we evaluated the extent and pattern of missingness. Rows with substantial missing economic data were removed to preserve the integrity of comparisons, while less critical missing values were handled through labeling explicitly as “UNKNOWN.” This approach ensured that we minimized data loss while maintaining transparency about incomplete records.

Another major data quality issue involved inconsistent data formats, which prevented straightforward processing and integration. One example was the date of birth (DOB) field in the ATP players dataset, which was stored as a floating-point number rather than a properly formatted date. Values such as “19900101.0” required multiple transformation steps before they could be interpreted correctly. Without changing their format, it would not have been possible to calculate derived variables such as player age, which was an important factor in our analysis. The ranking date field in the rankings dataset also needed to be standardized into a datetime format to allow for time-based comparisons and alignment with GDP data. These inconsistencies highlight a common issue in real-world datasets, where data collected from different sources or systems may not follow the same formatting conventions.

A particularly challenging issue arose from inconsistencies in country identifiers across datasets. The ATP players dataset used three-letter IOC country codes, while the GDP dataset used full country names. This mismatch created a barrier to merging the datasets, as there was no direct one-to-one correspondence between identifiers. Also, some countries were represented differently across datasets due to variations in naming conventions (e.g., abbreviations, alternative spellings, or historical names). To resolve this issue, we constructed a mapping between IOC codes and full country names, ensuring that each player could be accurately linked to the corresponding economic data. This process required careful validation to avoid incorrect matches, since even minor inconsistencies could lead to questionable merges and compromised results.

Another important data quality concern was the presence of outdated or invalid entries. The players dataset included records for countries that no longer exist, such as Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union. These entries posed a problem because the GDP dataset reflects modern country boundaries and does not include corresponding economic data for these. Including these records would have introduced inconsistencies and potentially skewed the analysis. As a result, these entries were removed to ensure alignment between datasets and to maintain relevance to current tennis rankings. Similarly, the GDP dataset contained aggregated regional entries, such as “Asia less Japan” or “Asia and Oceania,” which do not correspond to individual player nationalities. These entries were also removed to maintain a consistent level of granularity in the data.

Skewness in the data distributions was another significant issue we encountered. Both GDP and player ranking points were highly skewed distributions, with a small number of countries or players having extremely large values compared to the rest. This type of distribution can distort visualizations and statistical measures, making it difficult to identify meaningful patterns. For example, in raw scatter plots, the majority of data points were compressed into a narrow range, while a few extreme values dominated the scale. To address this, we applied log transformations to GDP values, which helped normalize the distribution and reveal underlying trends more clearly. Recognizing and addressing skewness was essential for ensuring that the analysis accurately reflected the relationships in the data.

A more conceptual data quality issue stemmed from differences in data granularity between datasets. The ATP players and rankings datasets operate at the individual level, capturing detailed information about each player, while the GDP dataset is aggregated at the country level. This mismatch introduces the risk of ecological fallacy, where conclusions about individual behavior are inferred from aggregate data. For example, even if a country has a high GDP, not all players from that country necessarily benefit equally from its economic resources. This limitation does not invalidate the analysis but requires careful interpretation of results. Throughout the project, we remained mindful of this issue and avoided making overly strong claims about causality.

Finally, the provenance of the data also influenced its quality. The ATP dataset was compiled by Jeff Sackmann from multiple sources, rather than being obtained directly from an official ATP database. While the dataset is widely used and generally reliable, the process of aggregation introduces the possibility of minor inconsistencies or errors. Similarly, the GDP dataset includes both observed and projected values, which may differ in accuracy. Understanding the origins of the data helped inform our approach to cleaning and analysis, as it highlighted the need for validation and cautious interpretation.

In summary, the data quality assessment revealed a range of issues typical of real-world datasets, including missing values, inconsistent formats, identifier mismatches, skewed distributions, outdated entries, and differences in granularity. By systematically identifying and addressing these issues, we were able to construct a clean and consistent dataset that supports meaningful analysis while also acknowledging the inherent limitations of the data.


## Data Cleaning
Our data cleaning process involved several steps to standardize and merge the disparate datasets into a cohesive whole. We first standardized the player date of birth column by converting floats to integers, then to strings, and finally parsing them into proper datetime objects to calculate player ages for the 2024 ranking dates. To bridge the gap between datasets, we created an extensive custom mapping dictionary to translate IOC country codes into the full country names used by the USDA Economic Research Service. We also filtered the GDP dataset to remove aggregated regional entries to ensure individual countries were matched correctly. Historical country entities that were irrelevant to 2024 rankings, such as Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union, were explicitly excluded from the analysis. For the remaining datasets, we performed a left merge on the player_id attribute to combine player attributes with their points and rankings. Following the merge, any remaining missing values in the combined dataframe were imputed with the label "UNKNOWN" for categorical fields or dropped if they were critical numerical values. This process was documented through Python scripts that profile and clean the data, ensuring that the transformation from raw CSVs to the final analytical dataset is transparent and repeatable. Each operation was designed to address a specific quality issue, such as resolving the DtypeWarning by standardizing column types and ensuring temporal consistency across all data points.

## Findings
Our analysis of the integrated data revealed a clear positive correlation between a country's wealth and the professional success of its tennis players. When visualizing the relationship, a log-scaled scatter plot of GDP against player points showed a distinct upward trend, effectively mitigating the extreme skewness found in raw GDP values. Categorical analysis further supported this, as box plots organized by wealth quartiles (Low, Mid-Low, Mid-High, and High) demonstrated that countries in the "High" GDP group have significantly higher median ranking points than those in the "Low" group. We also explored the distribution of player points and GDP separately, observing that both are highly skewed, which justifies the use of logarithmic transformations for more accurate modeling. The findings suggest that while height and age contribute to a player's potential, the national GDP acts as a significant predictor for the likelihood of a player reaching the upper echelons of the ATP rankings. Our linear regression model utilized these features to estimate ranking outcomes, revealing that national economic status is a substantial factor in explaining performance variance. Although socio-economic and physical factors influence a player's rank, the high degree of variance in the results suggests that individual talent and professional coaching continue to play critical roles. These visualizations and numeric results are saved in the project repository to support the transparency of our conclusions.

## Future Work
There are several lessons and potential avenues for expansion identified during this project. We learned that integrating data at different granularities, such as individual player metrics versus national economic indicators, requires meticulous mapping and careful cleaning to avoid ecological bias. Future iterations of this work could incorporate "Tournament Density" to see if a country's frequency of hosting professional events correlates with higher rankings for its citizens due to decreased travel stress and increased local support. Another interesting direction would be to analyze a country's wealth during a player's formative years, perhaps between the ages of five and fifteen, to see if early-life economic conditions are more predictive of success than current GDP. We could also investigate the influence of climate and weather patterns on training availability, as these factors might interact with national wealth. Additionally, expanding the feature set to include tournament surfaces (clay, grass, hard) or travel distance to major Grand Slam events could provide a more nuanced understanding of player success. Integrating coaching data or the presence of national tennis academies could further explain why some high-GDP countries perform better than others of similar wealth. These future steps would refine the predictive power of our models and offer a more comprehensive view of the professional tennis ecosystem.

## Challenges
The most significant challenge we faced was the complex data integration required to merge the GDP and tennis datasets. Mapping over 200 country entities that used different naming conventions and codes required extensive manual verification and the creation of a custom dictionary to ensure consistency. Feature engineering also proved difficult, particularly calculating player age because the birth dates were stored in a non-standard float format that required a multi-step conversion process. Furthermore, handling the heavily skewed distributions of both GDP and ranking points was a major hurdle for visualization and modeling. This necessitated the use of logarithmic transformations and the creation of categorical wealth bins to produce meaningful statistical results and clear visual representations of the underlying trends. Finally, removing regional economic aggregates and obsolete historical countries was necessary but time-consuming to ensure the data granularity remained consistent across the individual and national levels. Ensuring the reproducibility of these manual cleaning steps in a programmatic script required significant debugging, especially when handling the NaN values that emerged after the multi-table join. These challenges highlight the importance of systematic data curation in socio-economic sports analysis.
